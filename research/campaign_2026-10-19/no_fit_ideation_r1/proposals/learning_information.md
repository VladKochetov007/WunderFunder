# Independent proposals: learning and information

These are ten proposals for discussion, not measured results. The task facts used here are the two anonymous future indicators, causal 20,000-row sequences with 99 warm-up rows, 112 stable channel identities with no known depth ordering, no online labels or scoring mask, and `global_wp_clip2_v1`: clip labels and predictions to ±2, weight by absolute clipped label, pool moments globally per target, then average the two target correlations. No data scan or model work was done. External papers below establish only the described method in their own setting; none establishes transfer to Wunder.

## 1. Fit the metric's tilted conditional mean

For each target, let `y=clip(t,−2,2)` and `w=|y|`; minimize the population risk `E[w(p(X)−y)^2]`, with equal target-specific treatment. Under the label-tilted distribution `Q ∝ wP`, this estimates `E_Q[Y|X]=E_P[wY|X]/E_P[w|X]`. Among square-integrable functions of `X`, that conditional mean maximizes `Corr_Q(Y,g(X))` by Cauchy–Schwarz; this is a derivation of the score-relevant functional, not a claim that an arbitrary correlation loss beats MSE. The deficiency is that ordinary MSE estimates under `P`, while official WP uses `Q`. Assumption: training eligible rows represent scored-row selection. Prediction: a better high-|clipped-target| conditional direction. Cheapest check: verify the identity on a two-point symbolic example (no fit); later compare paired weighted and ordinary MSE fits. Falsifier: stable lower pooled WP. Strongest alternative is ordinary MSE, with lower weight variance; the proposed loss costs little but has lower effective sample size.

## 2. Correct minibatch bias from pooled WP moments

Official WP is one correlation over all selected rows per target; averaging minibatch correlations changes the objective because each batch has its own means and denominator. For unsaturated predictions under a fixed pooled weighted distribution, the exact per-row derivative is proportional to `w[(y−μy)/(σyσp)−ρ(p−μp)/σp²]`. A treatment would stream or periodically refresh pooled moments and use this derivative across sequence partitions, rather than treating each minibatch correlation as the target. Assumption: moments estimated on the training population are relevant to the evaluation population, and their refresh lag is short enough. Prediction: batch-size and partition changes cease to move the estimated score direction substantially. Cheapest check: derive and finite-difference this formula on a tiny synthetic pooled example (no task data); later compare pooled-gradient and minibatch-correlation recipes. Falsifier: no reproducible WP or stability gain after accounting for the extra passes. Strongest alternative is proposal 1's weighted MSE, whose population functional is exact and whose implementation is cheaper; pooled moments add forward passes, memory, and optimization noise.

## 3. Give both targets equal influence on shared capacity

The official final score is the arithmetic mean of two target correlations, but a shared trunk trained with a sum of raw-scale losses can spend capacity according to target variance or gradient size. Treatment: compute each target's pooled-WP gradient separately and combine the two shared-trunk gradients with fixed `1/2` weights; keep target heads separate. This implements the metric's explicit scalarization rather than applying generic gradient balancing. Assumption: the trunk is capacity-limited and the targets have some transferable signal; if there is no sharing bottleneck, this may do nothing. Prediction: one target's score is less likely to improve by sacrificing the other, and the aggregate WP rises or stays stable. Cheapest check: algebraically confirm that the combined objective is the official two-target mean (no fit); later compare with the current shared-loss recipe using paired seeds. Falsifier: aggregate WP is unchanged or falls while one target takes extra damage. Strongest alternative is separate trunks, which avoid interference but cost more parameters, fitting time, and inference state.

## 4. Sample by score-gradient influence without changing the objective

For weighted MSE, rare large `|clip(y)|` examples can contribute disproportionately to gradients. During fitting only, sample rows or causal blocks with probability related to an inexpensive estimate of weighted per-example gradient norm, then apply inverse-probability correction so the expected gradient still matches the chosen full-data risk. This is importance sampling, not group-DRO or a change to the target distribution. Assumption: gradient influence is concentrated enough that sampling reduces variance after index and correction overhead; `w` alone may be a poor norm proxy. Prediction: lower gradient variance or fewer updates to the same held-in risk at fixed compute, with no systematic objective drift. Cheapest check: derive variance on a two-stratum toy distribution (no fit); later record paired gradient variance and WP at fixed wall time. Falsifier: correction variance or sampling overhead erases the gain. The [ICML method](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/katharopoulos18a.html) estimates gradient-norm importance and gates it by predicted variance reduction, but its image/CNN/RNN results do not establish benefit for this metric or task. Uniform sampling is simpler; this adds sampler state and bookkeeping.

## 5. Use the 99 warm-up rows for label-free sequence normalization

At the start of each independent sequence, estimate robust per-channel location and scale from its 99 available warm-up rows; normalize subsequent current/past observations with those estimates and optionally update them causally. Do not key anything by `seq_ix`, use hidden `is_scored`, or use labels. This addresses possible sequence-to-sequence sensor offsets or scale shifts that a global normalizer cannot remove. Assumption: these shifts are nuisance and the input-target relation is otherwise stable; warm-up is representative enough. Prediction: reduced score dispersion across validation sequences, especially when raw channel distributions shift. Cheapest check: the protocol establishes warm-up availability; actual shift and quality need a later fit/validation comparison. Falsifier: warm-up estimates are unstable or normalization lowers pooled WP. Strongest alternative is fixed global normalization, which preserves absolute levels and has no per-sequence estimation noise. [AdaBN](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.04779) adapts normalization statistics across target domains in vision networks; that paper does not validate a 99-row sequential estimator or this regression task. Inference cost is small, but state and reset logic are added.

## 6. Parameterize causal memory with an orthonormal filter basis

Replace a freely learned long lag kernel on selected feature summaries with a small bank of causal Laguerre filters at several fixed decay rates, then fit a compact readout over their responses. This makes the temporal map linear in a smaller number of coefficients and represents a family of smooth, long-lived responses without prespecifying a target horizon. Assumption: useful dependence is reasonably smooth and stable over time; sharp isolated-lag effects may be poorly represented. Prediction: for equal compute, less seed variation or a smaller train-validation gap, not necessarily higher score. Cheapest no-fit check: compute approximation error of the basis for synthetic impulses and decays at several lags; later compare against an unconstrained temporal learner under matched capacity. Falsifier: signal depends on sharp, irregular lags and the basis loses WP. [Orthonormal-basis system identification](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0967-0661(02)00113-2) reports low-order models for systems with selected dynamics, including Laguerre suitability for a dominant damped time constant; market feature-target dynamics need not satisfy that condition. A compact recurrent or dense-lag model is the strongest alternative; filter-bank choice and initialization add modest implementation work.

## 7. Orthogonalize cross-instrument proxy information, but keep its direct path

Use past/current `i1` features as a possible proxy for shared market conditions: estimate nuisance projections of `i0` feature summaries and target on `i1`, learn an `i0`-specific residual predictor, and retain a separate `i1` prediction path. This tests whether collinear cross-instrument context obscures incremental `i0` information without assuming that `i1` is irrelevant. Assumption: the projection is stable and residual signal is learnable; `i1` may itself carry direct predictive information. Prediction: the residual branch adds WP beyond an `i1`-only baseline and is more stable than a joint unstructured fit. Cheapest check: verify the partialling-out identity on a synthetic linear proxy example (no fit); later use cross-fitted nuisance estimates and compare joint, proxy-only, and residual-plus-proxy predictors. Falsifier: residual input adds nothing or removes reliable direct signal. [Proxy-variable identification](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.08816) requires independent informative proxies and a rank condition for causal identification; this proposal is predictive, those conditions are unknown, and the paper supplies no task-gain evidence. A joint model is simpler and may already learn the decomposition; orthogonalization costs extra nuisance fits and inference branches.

## 8. Test a shared predictive subspace across the two targets

Fit a common score component `z(X)` with two target-specific loadings and small residual heads, so the predictor can share signal while preserving differences between `t0` and `t1`. The task describes both as indicators of the same instrument's future movement, so their overlap could reduce target-label noise or improve sample efficiency. Assumption: a substantial fraction of predictable information is shared; unknown horizons or definitions may instead make their signals different. Prediction: the shared component helps both targets and residual heads remain small, with lower seed spread than fully independent heads. Cheapest check: an algebraic synthetic example with one latent signal plus target-specific noise (no fit); later compare shared-only, shared-plus-residual, and independent heads. Falsifier: the target-specific residuals carry most of the score or shared training hurts one target enough to lower the mean. Independent heads are the strongest alternative and cost more capacity but impose fewer assumptions. The decomposition has a scale/sign non-identifiability, so fix one loading or normalize `z`; this is a proposal, not evidence that the labels share a latent factor.

## 9. Encode channel identity without inventing depth adjacency

For each known feature family, use shared per-channel transforms plus an explicit categorical identity embedding and symmetric aggregation/interactions; do not use a convolution that treats adjacent indices as neighboring book levels. The documentation guarantees stable columns but says indices are identifiers, not depth order. Assumption: channels in a family share some response structure after identity is represented; if each channel has unrelated semantics, sharing introduces bias. Prediction: predictions are unchanged by a storage permutation when values and identity tags move together, while channel-specific identity effects remain available. Cheapest check: prove the permutation property on a symbolic input (no fit); later compare with a channel-specific dense map and perturb only the storage order. Falsifier: the shared transform reduces WP or identity tags fail to preserve behavior. A full dense map is the strongest alternative, with higher parameter count and weaker inductive bias. [Deep Sets](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/f22e4747da1aa27e363d86d40ff442fe-Paper.pdf) characterizes permutation-invariant functions on genuine sets; these channels are stably labeled, so blindly discarding identity would be an invalid transfer. This proposal changes the representation, not backbone width or depth.

## 10. Separate persistent input level from causal innovation

For each channel, maintain a causal slow baseline (for example, a fixed-timescale exponential average) and expose both the baseline and the innovation `x_t−baseline_t` to the predictor. This tests a signal-processing hypothesis: persistent level shifts may obscure short-lived predictive changes, while the bypass preserves any useful level information. It does not create auxiliary future targets or require labels at inference. Assumption: useful information has separable slow and fast components and the baseline timescale is not disastrously mismatched. Prediction: the innovation path improves stability under offset drift while the baseline bypass prevents a loss when levels matter. Cheapest check: show algebraically that constant offsets cancel from the innovation but remain in the bypass (no fit); later compare raw-only, innovation-only, and both-path models across sequence-held-out folds. Falsifier: levels carry most predictive information or the estimated baseline lags regime changes. A raw-history model is simpler and more expressive; this adds one causal state per channel and a small timescale choice. No empirical claim is made.

## Questions for discussion

1. Should the exact tilted-MSE functional be the default low-cost objective, with pooled-WP gradients reserved for a capacity or finite-sample failure that MSE cannot address?
2. Is `i1` better treated first as a proxy for shared conditions, as a direct predictive input, or both through the proposed residual-plus-bypass design?
3. Are sequence-level distribution shifts plausible enough that 99 warm-up rows can estimate useful normalization, given the risk that warm-up statistics are noisy or predictive levels are removed?
