# Round 3: canonical descriptive results and mechanism decisions

Date: 2026-10-17. This update incorporates the corrected full-census exception results, the canonical scoring retry, exact target/mask/order validation for the shared panel, the six-sum sequence decomposition, and the follow-up cross-model/age-stratum diagnostics. I did not run a scan, model inference, fit, or optimizer. The saved-array transform scores and decomposition now match the canonical centered scorer; historical prediction-cache producer linkage remains unknown, so the arrays are not certified as outputs from the currently inspected source/checkpoints. The priority decisions below are consequently descriptive and conditional, not claims of a current-model causal effect or expected deployment gain.

## What the records establish

The census contains 941 train exception rows across 688 sequences and 312 valid exception rows across 203 sequences. Each is a raw target change outside the selected `i0_p0 AND i0_p11` change clock. Every exception is followed by an AND-clock event: within 1–3 rows in train and 1–2 rows in valid, with median offset 1 in both splits. The observed counterexamples therefore look like short timing mismatches around the next joint price-code event, not long stretches of target evolution with no later event. That makes an exact same-row clock false while preserving the clock as a plausible approximate summary. The corrected full-census aggregates and definitions are in the [exception results](../exception_audit/results.json).

The raw changes are not all numerical dust: the minimum over exception rows of the largest absolute change across the two targets is 0.125432968, and none is at or below (10^{-4}). At the same time, 63 train and 23 valid raw exceptions have no change in either clipped target. The corrected counts show 92 scored raw exceptions but 85 scored clipped exceptions, so exactly 7 of the scored raw exceptions do not change either clipped target. These rows are real raw-label changes but not scored-label transitions under the ([-2,2]) target clipping rule.

There are 92 scored valid exception rows. Their combined official target-weight mass is 155.2803989, against 4,895,308.008 across the R1 valid panel: 0.003172%. This is strong evidence that these particular rows are not a large share of total score weight. It is not a direct WP delta, a residual-weighted influence estimate, or a strict claim that their possible leverage is zero. The full-valid clock-reconstruction diagnostic adds a related but distinct quantity: replaying the clipped label only at each AND event leaves 61 scored rows mismatched for target 0 and 59 for target 1; the per-target mismatch-row weight shares are 0.001874% and 0.002030%. For the broader need-eligible valid reconstruction, mismatch episodes have median length one row and maximum length two; score-mask-specific episode lengths were not reported. The two target counts may overlap, so they are not a unique-row total. These measures say the scored label-mismatch weight is small, not that a label-clock reconstruction is an acceptable predictor or that model residual influence is exactly zero.

The strongest defensible interpretation is now narrower than “the clock misses persistent target updates”: target changes do sometimes precede the next AND event, usually by one row, but the scored exception weight is tiny in aggregate. Timing offset, feature asynchrony, and the hidden-state/readout response remain alternative explanations. No observation here says whether other allowed features move on each exception, whether an incumbent predicts the change, or whether a different output changes global WP.

## What this does not decide

The records falsify only the exact implication “every raw target change occurs on an AND-clock row.” They do **not** show that a useful predictor loses information on an exception, that freezing hidden updates between AND events lowers WP, or that event features are unhelpful. A counterexample to a target-clock identity is not evidence of practical score damage. The underlying recurrent models consume every row; output-hold diagnostics change saved emissions while leaving those hidden updates intact. A loss from output holding would therefore show that this emission policy damages these fixed predictions, not that hidden-state freezing has the same effect. A gain would show an emission opportunity, not that a frozen state is safe.

The short offset shifts the next useful question from “how can the model survive long unmarked target motion?” to “is there a reproducible, forecastable one-to-three-row lead/lag around the selected event clock, and does it matter to a current predictor?” That question still requires aligned fixed-model residuals and other-feature measurements. The 92-row weight mass is too small to justify a clock-exception-specific architecture as the campaign's main route without unusually concentrated residual influence.

The label-persistence diagnostics are separate from prediction quality. Event offsets alone do not quantify cumulative target-clock reconstruction error; the corrected reconstruction now does so descriptively, with very small scored weight shares. None of these label-only numbers estimates a Bayes ceiling or what a causal model could know in advance.

The roughly +0.029 to +0.030 oracle gains are a separate signal: for these predictions, future-defined averages within constant **clipped target** runs remove substantial prediction variance while preserving weighted covariance, so WP rises. This oracle sees the future label-run boundaries and future predictions. It shows that per-row causal filtering is a poor proxy for the retrospective operation: holds and EMAs blur row-wise revisions without knowing the label-run boundary, while the oracle is allowed to know exactly where each run starts and ends. It is not a public gain, a causal candidate, or a Bayes ceiling. “All simple causal holds/smoothers lose” and “future-label averaging gains” are compatible; this pattern motivates tests of how per-row predictions respond around transitions, but does not by itself validate a boundary-aware mechanism.

Do not center on each sequence's true target mean at deployment.

## Follow-up: cross-model agreement and age-by-class strata

On the same 467-group / 846,654-row panel, all pairwise weighted correlations are high across the three saved arrays: clipped predictions correlate 0.99089–0.99253 and clipped residuals correlate 0.99017–0.99219. The residuals share the same target (Y), so their high correlation is not an independent replication of a common model error; the common target contributes shared variation. Within constant clipped-label runs, prediction fluctuations are less similar but still strongly correlated across pairs, 0.960421–0.963201. These fluctuations are defined after subtracting each sequence/run's selected-row mean using retrospective clipped-label run boundaries, so they are diagnostics rather than online signals. An algebraic equal-weight-mean variance ratio derived from the stored covariance sums is 0.974492594 for target 0 and 0.974932436 for target 1: averaging these three fluctuation series would retain about 97.5% of their mean individual variance. No ensemble prediction was formed or graded.

This weakens the explanation that the oracle's roughly +0.03 WP gain mostly removes independent seed noise. These related arrays show little low-correlation fluctuation for a simple ensemble to cancel. It does **not** show that common fluctuations are useful, nor that they are an avoidable common bias: shared input response, shared model/data structure, a common error, and partly common target-driven residual covariance remain distinct explanations. The same common-mode result does not reverse the output-transform finding—AND/OR holds, cadence and EMA still reduce WP on the fixed arrays—and it does not make the retrospective oracle deployable. The correlations are conditional on arrays whose producer order is unverified and include a tied-lead model family alongside two t384 seeds; they are not a controlled seed-replication study. See [follow-up diagnostics](../prediction_audit/FOLLOWUP_DIAGNOSTICS.json).

The follow-up age-by-market-class MSE grid is heterogeneous, with many thin class/age/target cells. As one example, for t384 seed 3, the retrospective `quantum_3` class at AND-age `[512,20001)` has 11,699 selected rows and target-0 weighted MSE 2.1559 for baseline versus 2.5079 for AND-hold. This is an illustrative cell, not evidence that a causal age gate works or that the hold loss grows monotonically with age. `market_class` is target-derived and retrospective; age is left-censored at sequence reset. Also, `weighted_target_variance_sum` in this report is a sum of within-sequence centered variances across sequences in that stratum, not the pooled stratum target variance; the weighted MSE numerator and denominator are unchanged. The [variance-field clarification](../prediction_audit/AGE_CLASS_VARIANCE_CLARIFICATION.json) records that distinction. No age-by-class result supplies a recoverable ceiling or a deployment feature.

The AND-hold result has at least two competing explanations that these numbers cannot separate. It may be that post-event rows contain useful new observations, so using only the event-time output loses them. Or the recurrent state/readout may be settling after the event even under little new information, so the first-event output is transient and a hold misses the later settled forecast. A third route is the existing current-row encoder skip: the main `RNNModel` readout concatenates the GRU output with current encoder projection `z` (`src/wnn/nn.py`, `RNNModel.forward`), so its prediction can respond directly to a new row even apart from accumulated recurrent history. None is established by the output-hold contrast, because the baseline continues both state updates and ordinary emission.

An archive-first prior audit makes generic multirate memory a poor novelty claim. The [October 8 review](</Users/nb/Documents/wunder/research-2026-10-07/execution/NEXT_MULTIRATE_MEMORY_REVIEW.md>) found that a DualClock with a dense row GRU plus an event-updated GRU over projected row context and log gap had already been trained; its early E-056 comparison was 0.6305 versus 0.6310 for the row-GRU control. Later four-epoch EMA scores were 0.6877 versus 0.6879 and quiet-class-3 scores 0.3946 versus 0.4020. A later composite with a fixed 0.2 replacement improved valid by 0.000459 and train-HO by 0.000021, but that limited positive result does not erase the earlier mixed/negative evidence or establish replication. The same review records an event-or-row%8 update comparison below the every-row control on both panels, with both intervals crossing zero, and an accumulated interval-memory comparison near zero. The current `TwoClockModel` source is consistent with that interpretation: the row GRU remains dense, while a held event-state and elapsed-age signal join row output and current `z` in the head (`src/wnn/nn.py`, `TwoClockModel`). Thus “dense observer plus a slow event memory,” or generic periodic/event-clock slow updates, is already explored. A soft gate on the *main* recurrent write is structurally different from an auxiliary event GRU, but it is not a new multirate-memory family and must beat a carefully matched recurrence control to support a state-update claim.

A useful next **no-fit** discriminator, only after binding each cache to its exact checkpoint and preprocessing source, is a paired frozen-checkpoint suffix replay. At selected AND events, fork from the identical saved event-time hidden state and compare actual post-event observations with a controlled counterfactual while scoring the same suffix rows. The intervention must specify its level: raw 112 input columns, the derived 99 event features, or the full 211-dimensional model input. Repeating the full 211-vector would also freeze age/event-derived features and could break the original causal feature-builder semantics. Prefer an intervention at the raw 112 level followed by the exact causal feature pipeline; use separately specified derived-feature arms only when their values can be rebuilt causally and consistently. Continue recurrent updates in each branch so a held-observation arm measures state/readout settling under repeated input; an optional constant-state readout is a distinct third reference if the model supports it cleanly. The `RNNModel` already has a current encoder-projection skip, so this is not a test of a missing fast raw-input path. It is not run, and input repetition is a counterfactual rather than an on-distribution model estimate. If actual suffixes outperform repeated raw inputs, that supports incremental information in post-event observations conditional on the same starting state; if their forecasts are similar but differ from a constant-state reference, recurrent settling is more plausible. Neither alone proves a public-score gain or mechanism causality.

## Remaining decision points

1. **Bind the prediction arrays to their producer before a hidden-state replay.** The alignment validator established that the target cache, targets, masks, sequence IDs and within-sequence steps match the Parquet rows exactly. It did not establish the historical prediction arrays' producer row order or link them to the three inspected checkpoints; their `lookahead=""` settings are useful but do not repair that missing receipt. If the cache producer cannot be recovered, interpret this panel as an opaque saved-output diagnostic. A future replay should use a source-bound checkpoint and the exact causal preprocessing pipeline.
2. **Join exception/reconstruction rows to fixed-model residuals and permitted input changes.** The record census and target-only reconstruction establish rarity and short label-clock error episodes, but they do not show which observations or incumbent forecast errors explain those changes. A repeatable, high-influence residual pattern accompanied by permitted features moving before the next AND event could motivate a narrow feature-conditioned update test. The 0.003172% exception weight and 0.001874%/0.002030% scored reconstruction-mismatch weight shares make any such promotion require an explicit influence analysis, not a row-count argument. Separately, the high within-run cross-model correlation weakens a generic “independent seed jitter” rationale for a smoother; any proposed causal filter must identify which allowed observations justify changing this shared component and test that on independent data.
3. **Use source-qualified paired evaluation for any future candidate.** The transform intervals are paired chain-bootstrap intervals conditional on three fixed arrays and one previously used fold. They quantify stability over the sampled chain components, not training-seed variation, checkpoint selection, fresh validation performance or public-score transfer. The standing full comparison is30 paired cells per arm across the four already exposed folds with shared-seed uncertainty and clean ancestry; it must not be relabeled a fresh holdout. Any genuinely new eligible evaluation would be additional evidence, not something this audit has created.

No card is promoted by the exception counts alone. The exact same-row target-clock identity is refuted and cumulative clipped-label reconstruction error is now quantified as small in scored weight; the approximate-clock question remains open. Dense output emissions outperform the tested simple hold/cadence/EMA policies on these saved arrays, but practical harm from hidden-state freezing remains untested. Cross-model agreement weakens an independent-seed-noise account of the retrospective oracle gap without proving a common correctable bias; the age-by-class table supplies no causal age gate.
